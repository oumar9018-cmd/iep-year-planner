IEP meeting checklist for Connecticut parents

One page to prepare with, take into the room, and work through afterwards. Print it, tick the boxes, and keep it with your copy of the IEP.

Connecticut's dates, in brief

Initial evaluation: 45 school days — The clock starts when the school receives the referral — not when you sign consent.
Annual review: at least once every 12 months · Reevaluation: at least once every 3 years · Transition planning in the IEP from age 14.

Before the meeting

Prepare

  • Ask for the paperwork early. Ask the school to share the draft IEP and any new evaluation reports before the meeting, so you can read them at home instead of under pressure.

  • Re-read the current IEP. Goals, service minutes, accommodations — know what was promised last time before you discuss the next one.

  • Check the progress reports. For each goal, ask: is my child actually progressing? Bring the reports that show it — or the ones that don't.

  • Write your parent concerns down. Your concerns must be considered by the team. Writing them down means they are on the record, word for word.

  • Gather your records. Report cards, work samples, private evaluations, medical notes — anything that shows what your child can do and where they struggle.

  • Know your dates. Your annual review deadline and your reevaluation date frame the whole meeting. Work them out before you walk in.

  • Bring a second pair of ears. You may bring a person who knows your child or has special expertise about them. Tell the school beforehand, as a courtesy.

During the meeting

In the room

  • Ask who everyone is. Names and roles. You should know exactly who decides what for your child.

  • Ask for present levels in numbers. Reading level, test scores, classroom performance — plain numbers, not general impressions.

  • Every goal must be measurable. For each goal ask: how will we know when this is met? If nobody can answer, the goal is not measurable yet.

  • Get the minutes in writing. How many minutes, how often, where, and group or one-to-one — the IEP should say all of it, because those minutes are a promise.

  • Ask what happens if it slips. If a goal stalls or sessions are missed, what happens next? Ask in the room, and note the answer.

  • Take notes. Write down what was agreed and what was refused. If you want to record the meeting, ask first — the rules vary.

  • You do not have to agree on the spot. You can ask for time to review the document at home. Ask what your signature means on each page — attendance and agreement are not always the same thing.

After the meeting

Follow through

  • Get your copy. The final IEP and the prior written notice are yours — ask for them before you leave, or for the date they will arrive.

  • Calendar the deadlines. Annual review, reevaluation, transition milestones — put them somewhere that counts down for you.

  • Log the minutes every week. Promised versus delivered, week by week. A simple log is the strongest evidence there is if minutes go missing.

  • Keep everything in one place. Every notice, report and IEP in one folder. Paper trails win disputes.

Questions parents ask

IEP meetings in Connecticut — FAQ

How often must the IEP team meet in Connecticut?

At least once a year — the annual review. But you can ask for an IEP meeting at any time during the year, in writing, if something changes or a goal stalls. The team must also reevaluate at least once every three years.

Can I bring someone with me to the IEP meeting?

Yes. You may bring a person who has knowledge or special expertise about your child — a family member, an advocate, a therapist, or a lawyer. Telling the school beforehand is a courtesy that keeps the meeting calm.

Do I have to sign the IEP at the meeting?

You can ask for time to read the document at home first. Ask what each signature line means — on some forms you are signing attendance, on others agreement. If you disagree with part of the IEP, say so in writing on the form or in a letter you keep a copy of.
